History Page

Story of Formula 1

From roaring V12s to today's hybrid engines — F1's history is a story of speed, innovation, and human courage that continues to write new chapters every season.

From Silverstone to a Global Phenomenon

On May 13, 1950, at Silverstone, the FIA Formula 1 World Championship was officially born. Italian driver Giuseppe Farina won the inaugural race and became the first World Champion.

The Italian Era (1950–1957)

Early F1 was dominated by Italian teams — Alfa Romeo, Ferrari, and Maserati. Juan Manuel Fangio became F1's first superstar, winning five World Championships with three different teams, a record that stood for nearly 50 years.

The Rise of British Teams (1958–1970s)

In 1958, the Constructors' Championship was introduced. Colin Chapman of Lotus revolutionized the sport with the monocoque chassis and ground effect aerodynamics. Jackie Stewart won three World Championships and became a pioneer of circuit safety.

The Golden Era (1980–1990s)

The 1980s witnessed the legendary rivalry between Ayrton Senna and Alain Prost. Honda's turbo engines dominated the era. In 1994, Senna tragically died at Imola, leading to major safety reforms in F1.

The Ferrari Dynasty (2000s)

Michael Schumacher joined Ferrari in 1996. From 2000 to 2004, he and Ferrari achieved five consecutive championships, and Schumacher claimed his seventh World Title — a record later matched by Lewis Hamilton in 2020.

The Hybrid Era (2010s–Present)

In 2014, F1 entered the V6 turbo hybrid era. Mercedes and Lewis Hamilton dominated the sport. In 2021, Max Verstappen ended Mercedes' reign. From 2022 to 2024, Verstappen and Red Bull achieved four consecutive titles. In 2025, F1 celebrates its 75th anniversary, with new manufacturers like Audi and Cadillac joining the grid.

Technology Page

Technology of Formula 1 car

Aerodynamics
The Heart of Speed
F1 cars are designed to generate massive downforce through front wings, rear wings, and diffusers, effectively gluing the car to the track. In 2026, active aerodynamics will replace the current DRS system, with "corner mode" and "straight mode" for optimal performance. "Dirty air" — turbulent airflow from the car ahead — remains a key challenge for overtaking.
Power Unit
From V8 to Hybrid
Today's F1 cars use a 1.6L V6 turbo hybrid engine producing nearly 1,000 horsepower with over 50% thermal efficiency. The MGU-K recovers braking energy, while the battery stores power for strategic deployment. In 2026, the electric output will increase by nearly 300%, achieving a 50:50 power split between the internal combustion engine and electric motor, powered by 100% sustainable fuels.
Chassis & Bodywork
The Miracle of Carbon Fiber
The monocoque is the driver's survival cell. Made of carbon fiber, it weighs just 35kg but is twice as strong as steel. The Halo is a titanium protection device weighing 7kg that can withstand 12 tonnes of impact. It has saved multiple lives since 2018. The roll hoop protects the driver's head in a rollover. Drivers are secured by a six-point harness, and the wheel is removable for emergency escape. The suspension uses push-rod or pull-rod design to control attitude, absorb shocks, and maintain grip.
Suspension & Tires
The Art of Grip
F1 cars use advanced push-rod and pull-rod suspension systems to optimize tire contact with the track. Pirelli provides five tire compounds ranging from hard to soft, with drivers forced to use at least two compounds per race. Tire temperature management is crucial for performance, with blankets heating tires to around 100°C before they hit the track.
Electronics & Safety
The Brains Behind the Beast
The steering wheel features over 20 buttons, allowing drivers to adjust engine modes, brake balance, and differential settings on the fly. The ECU (Electronic Control Unit) monitors hundreds of sensors, ensuring optimal performance. Safety advancements include the Halo cockpit protection, advanced crash structures, and the HANS device, all contributing to the sport's significantly improved safety record.

Classic Formula 1 Tracks All Seasons

Formula 1 circuits are FIA-homologated closed-loop racing venues, categorized into permanent purpose-built racetracks and temporary street circuits, spanning all five continents. Lap lengths typically range from 3.5 to 7 kilometers, with a minimum width of 12 meters, and must be equipped with pit lanes, safety barriers, and medical facilities.
